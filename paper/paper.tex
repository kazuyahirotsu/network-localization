\begin{abstract}
LPWA networks such as LoRa are often deployed for sensing, yet turning these deployments into localization infrastructure requires accurate anchor coordinates, a significant challenge in GNSS-denied or degraded environments. This work addresses the self-localization of static beacons in sparse outdoor networks using only Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) data. We propose a physics-aware, edge-conditioned GCN that learns a trainable RSSI-to-distance module jointly with graph message passing while treating anchor positions as fixed. This architecture uses edge features composed of RSSI time series and the learned distance estimate, enabling distance-aware aggregation without a fixed path-loss model. We build a terrain-aware Longley--Rice simulator and evaluate on 200 held-out graphs of 64 nodes (16 anchors) with 10 RSSI samples per directed link. In the terrain-aware regime, our method attains a mean error of 206.74 m, substantially outperforming a plain GCN (750.87 m) and iterative multilateration (1974.40 m). Under idealized free-space propagation, multilateration achieves 6.28 m mean error while our model reaches 51.15 m, demonstrating that terrain effects can substantially change the performance ranking of localization methods and underscoring the necessity of terrain-aware evaluation. Combining this infrastructure self-localization with client-side UAV positioning, we show that 200 m beacon error produces only 157 m mean UAV localization error, and closed-loop navigation simulations with a 200 m waypoint acceptance radius achieve 100\% waypoint success across diverse mission profiles. These results indicate a practical path toward coarse outdoor localization when GNSS is unavailable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{L}{ow-power} wide-area (LPWA) networks like LoRa are widely deployed for connecting battery-powered sensors for applications ranging from environmental monitoring to infrastructure sensing. While these networks excel at data collection, they hold underexploited potential as localization infrastructure. Repurposing a sensing network for localization, however, requires precise anchor node coordinates. In GNSS-denied or degraded settings, including urban canyons, dense foliage, and environments with intentional interference, acquiring these coordinates is a fundamental challenge that hinders the delivery of dependable location services.

This paper addresses the problem of self-localization for static LoRa beacons within a sparse, 4 km $\times$ 4 km outdoor network, using only Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) measurements. We consider a challenging yet practical scenario, namely a graph of 64 nodes with a 1:3 anchor-to-unknown ratio (16 anchors, 48 unknowns), where each directed link provides a limited budget of 10 RSSI samples. The objective is to infer the absolute 2D positions of the unknown nodes under realistic LoRa operating conditions without relying on high-precision timing or extensive site surveys.

In this paper, we demonstrate that a physics-aware, edge-conditioned graph convolutional network (GCN) can recover beacon locations with sufficient accuracy for downstream navigation tasks, even in these sparse, terrain-rich environments. Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A novel GCN architecture that integrates a trainable, physically-interpretable RSSI-to-distance module. Its parameters are learned end-to-end with the graph network, producing distance-aware edge features that adapt to the environment.
    \item An anchor-aware message-passing scheme that leverages connectivity between all nodes (including unknown-to-unknown links) to perform collective inference, while keeping anchor positions fixed to prevent geometric drift.
    \item A rigorous, terrain-aware evaluation pipeline using a Longley-Rice propagation model, which demonstrates the proposed model's robustness and reveals how idealized free-space assumptions can produce misleading performance rankings.
    \item A UAV localization and navigation feasibility study that quantifies how beacon position errors propagate to UAV localization and demonstrates navigation feasibility through closed-loop simulations using the client-side method from \cite{r1}.
\end{itemize}

Our approach differs from prior work by combining explicit physical modeling with graph-based learning. Unlike multilateration, which struggles with the ill-posed RSSI-to-distance inversion in complex terrain, our method uses a learned distance as a soft prior. Unlike fingerprinting, it requires no laborious site surveys. Unlike other GNN-based methods that treat the network as a black box, our model introduces a trainable RSSI-to-distance module to inject a physical prior into the graph and is specifically validated for sparse, outdoor LPWA topologies where timing-based methods are often impractical.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~II reviews related work in localization. Section~III presents the proposed method, while Section~IV details the evaluation setup and baselines. Section~V reports quantitative and qualitative results. Section~VI evaluates how self-localization errors propagate to UAV positioning and demonstrates navigation feasibility. Section~VII discusses performance drivers and limitations, and Section~VIII concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
We review classical geometric localization and graph-based deep learning, focusing on the methods and data modalities relevant to outdoor, GNSS-denied localization.

\subsection{Localization Methods}
Classical multilateration and its iterative variants form the foundation of geometric localization, recovering node positions from pairwise distances using solvers like least-squares \cite{r2,r3}. Iterative multilateration improves coverage by temporarily promoting newly localized nodes to anchor status. However, as noted in \cite{r4}, this strategy is vulnerable to error propagation in sparse graphs with limited line-of-sight, where early inaccuracies in promoted nodes can compound catastrophically. 

Fingerprinting methods learn a direct mapping from signal observations (e.g., RSSI vectors) to coordinates. While effective in constrained environments, this approach requires extensive, laborious site surveys to build the signal map and is sensitive to environmental changes, limiting its practicality for large-scale outdoor deployments \cite{r5,r6}.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful approach for localization by explicitly modeling the network as a graph, which allows them to leverage connectivity between unknown nodes, a source of information that geometric solvers cannot use. Prior work has demonstrated the value of GNNs for various localization tasks, including indoor localization with high-order GNNs \cite{r7} and graph-fusion designs \cite{r8}, large-scale network localization \cite{r9}, and indoor positioning using WiFi RSSI \cite{r10}. Our work builds directly on the principles of edge-conditioned convolutions, as proposed in \cite{r11}, where each edge generates its own weight matrix from its attributes so that different neighbors contribute differently. However, previous GNN applications have primarily focused on indoor environments or have not integrated an explicit, trainable physical propagation model. Our key distinction is the introduction of a physics-aware RSSI-to-distance module, which provides an interpretable prior that is learned jointly with the graph network, a technique specifically tailored for sparse, outdoor LPWA topologies.

\subsection{Communication Data}
Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) is the most widely available metric on commodity LPWA radios, making it a common feature for both multilateration and learning-based methods \cite{r2}. However, RSSI is notoriously noisy and its relationship with distance is heavily dependent on terrain and obstacles.

Time-based methods, such as Time of Flight (ToF) or TDoA, offer a more direct path to distance estimation by measuring signal propagation time \cite{r12,r13}. However, these techniques require tightly synchronized clocks, which are typically achieved using the very GNSS systems we aim to supplement. Achieving this synchronization in GNSS-denied settings is often impractical or prohibitively expensive, limiting the feasibility of these methods for low-cost, large-scale deployments.

Channel State Information (CSI) provides much more fine-grained, physical-layer information than RSSI and has been successfully used for high-accuracy indoor localization \cite{r8}. However, the specialized hardware required to extract CSI is not commonly available on low-cost outdoor LPWA devices.

Given these practical constraints, we focus on an RSSI-only approach that is compatible with existing, widely-deployed hardware.

\subsection{Relation to Our Previous Work}
Our previous work \cite{r1} focuses on the localization of a mobile drone relative to a set of beacons with known, fixed positions, using a robust multilateration algorithm that combines the Huber loss function with altitude constraints to suppress RSSI outliers. In contrast, the present work addresses a different and complementary problem, the self-localization of the static beacons themselves, using only pairwise RSSI measurements within the beacon network. The two methods form a complete positioning pipeline. The GCN proposed here estimates beacon positions, which then serve as the reference infrastructure for the client-side UAV localization of \cite{r1}. In Section~VI, we evaluate this complete pipeline to quantify how self-localization errors propagate to downstream UAV positioning and to assess navigation feasibility.

\section{Proposed Method}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_01} provides an overview of the model and data flow.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
We model the beacon network as a directed graph $G=(\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ with a set of anchors $\mathcal{A}$ and unknown nodes $\mathcal{U}$, where $|\mathcal{V}|=64$ and $|\mathcal{A}|=16$. Each node $i$ has an absolute 2D position $\mathbf{p}_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$, which is known for $i \in \mathcal{A}$ and unknown for $i \in \mathcal{U}$. For each directed edge $(i, j) \in \mathcal{E}$, we observe a time series of $K=10$ RSSI samples, $\{r_{ij}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^K$.

We adopt a log-distance path-loss model to relate RSSI to geometric distance:
\begin{equation}
r_{ij}^{(k)} = P_t - 10 n \log_{10}(d_{ij}) + o + \varepsilon_{ij}^{(k)}
\label{eq:pathloss_model}
\end{equation}
where $d_{ij} = ||\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j||_2$ is the Euclidean distance. $P_t$ is the effective transmit power, $n$ is the path-loss exponent, $o$ is an offset, and $\varepsilon_{ij}^{(k)}$ represents noise. From the sample mean $\bar{r}_{ij}$, we form a per-edge distance estimate:
\begin{equation}
\hat{d}_{ij} = 10^{((P_t + o - \bar{r}_{ij})/(10 n))}
\label{eq:rssi_to_dist}
\end{equation}
In our implementation, $P_t$ is fixed at 13 dBm, while $n$ and $o$ are learned parameters.

The initial node features are defined as $\mathbf{x}_i^{(0)} = [x_i, y_i, \text{is\_anchor}]$, where $(x_i, y_i)$ are the known coordinates for anchors and are initialized at the anchor centroid for unknown nodes. The edge attributes for an edge $(i,j)$ are $\mathbf{e}_{ij} = [r_{ij}^{(1)}, \dots, r_{ij}^{(K)}, \hat{d}_{ij}]$. The model takes these node features and edge attributes as input and outputs the predicted absolute positions $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_i$ for all $i \in \mathcal{U}$. Training and evaluation use synthetic graph instances generated from a terrain-aware propagation simulator, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\subsection{Key Challenges}
Infrastructure self-localization faces several challenges beyond standard localization:

\textit{Sparse Anchor Information:} With limited anchors, many unknown nodes have no direct anchor connectivity. Information must propagate through unknown-to-unknown links, but these links carry measurement noise.

\textit{Error Propagation:} Classical iterative methods that promote newly localized nodes to anchor status suffer from error propagation, where small errors in early localizations compound through subsequent iterations, potentially causing catastrophic failure.

\textit{Terrain Effects:} Outdoor deployment in mountainous or urban terrain introduces severe signal attenuation that violates simple path-loss models. The RSSI-distance relationship becomes noisy and multi-modal.

\subsection{Physics-Aware Edge-Conditioned GCN}
Standard graph convolutions apply identical transformation weights to all edges, treating messages from all neighbors equally. This is problematic for localization, where the reliability of a neighbor's message depends on the edge characteristics, which reflect the propagation environment between nodes. We employ edge-conditioned convolutions (NNConv) \cite{r11} where a small neural network, the EdgeNet, dynamically generates a unique weight matrix for each edge based on its attributes. A layer's computation is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_i^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma\left( \text{mean}_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \left( \mathbf{W}_{ij}^{(\ell)} \mathbf{x}_j^{(\ell)} \right) \right)
\label{eq:nnconv}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}(i)$ denotes the neighbors of node $i$, $\mathbf{W}_{ij}^{(\ell)} = \text{EdgeNet}(\mathbf{e}_{ij})$ is the edge-specific weight matrix generated from edge features, and $\sigma$ is the ReLU activation. Mean aggregation is used to combine messages from all neighbors.

\paragraph{EdgeNet Architecture}
The EdgeNet is a 2-layer MLP that maps the $(K{+}1)$-dimensional edge feature vector $\mathbf{e}_{ij}$ (10 RSSI samples plus 1 distance estimate) to a flattened weight matrix of size $H_{\text{in}} \times H_{\text{out}}$:
\begin{equation}
\text{EdgeNet}: \mathbb{R}^{K+1} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{H_{\text{in}} \times H_{\text{out}}}
\end{equation}
Each EdgeNet contains 64 hidden units with ReLU activation. The output is reshaped into the weight matrix $\mathbf{W}_{ij}$. This architecture enables the network to learn which edges provide more reliable information. For instance, the network can learn to trust edges with stable RSSI measurements (low variance across the $K$ samples) more than edges with high variance, which may indicate multipath or obstruction.

\paragraph{Network Structure}
The full network consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Input layer: 3-dimensional node features $(x, y, \text{is\_anchor})$
    \item First NNConv layer: $3 \rightarrow 64$ dimensions with ReLU activation
    \item Second NNConv layer: $64 \rightarrow 64$ dimensions with ReLU activation
    \item Output layer: Linear mapping from $64 \rightarrow 2$ (predicted $x, y$ coordinates)
\end{enumerate}
Each NNConv layer has its own EdgeNet with the same architecture but separate learned parameters.

\subsection{Trainable Path-Loss Module}
Rather than using fixed path-loss parameters from literature or manual calibration, we learn the global parameters $n$ (path-loss exponent) and $o$ (offset) as trainable scalar parameters optimized jointly with the GCN weights. The transmit power $P_t$ is fixed at 13 dBm, as it is typically specified by the radio hardware.

\paragraph{Numerical Stability Constraints}
To ensure numerical stability during training, we apply several constraints that prevent invalid or extreme distance estimates:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Denominator clamping:} The term $10n$ is clamped to $|10n| \geq \epsilon_n$ to prevent division by values near zero when the path-loss exponent is small.
    \item \textbf{Exponent clamping:} The exponent $(P_t + o - \bar{r}_{ij}) / (10n)$ is clamped to the range $[-2, 5]$, corresponding to plausible distances from 0.01 m to 100 km given typical RSSI ranges.
    \item \textbf{Distance lower bound:} The final distance estimate $\hat{d}_{ij}$ is lower-bounded by a small $\epsilon > 0$ to prevent zero or negative distances.
\end{itemize}
These constraints are essential because outlier RSSI values or unstable parameter values during early training can produce invalid distance estimates that destabilize optimization. The constraint ranges are chosen to be permissive enough not to affect valid links within the 4 km evaluation area while preventing numerical issues.

\paragraph{Benefits of Learned Parameters}
Learning path-loss parameters jointly with the GCN provides two key benefits:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Environment Adaptation:} The model automatically adapts to the propagation characteristics of the training environment, learning appropriate values of $n$ and $o$ for the terrain and conditions.
    \item \textbf{Interpretability:} After training, the learned parameters can be examined to understand the effective propagation model, providing insight into the environment (e.g., higher $n$ values indicate more severe path loss due to terrain).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Anchor Handling}
Anchor nodes serve as fixed reference points that prevent the solution from drifting or scaling arbitrarily. We implement anchor handling through three mechanisms:

\paragraph{Fixed Coordinates}
Anchor coordinates are treated as ground truth throughout training and inference. While messages are passed through all nodes (including anchors), the predicted coordinates for anchors are overwritten with their known values after each forward pass.

\paragraph{Loss Exclusion}
Anchors are excluded from the loss calculation, ensuring gradients flow only from unknown node predictions.

\paragraph{Message Passing Participation}
Despite having fixed outputs, anchors participate fully in message passing. This allows anchor position information to propagate to neighboring unknown nodes, providing geometric constraints throughout the graph.

\subsection{Training Objective}
The network is trained to minimize the Smooth L1 loss between predicted and true coordinates for unknown nodes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{U}} \rho(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_i - \mathbf{p}_i)
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the Smooth L1 function:
\begin{equation}
\rho(x) = \begin{cases}
    0.5 x^2 & \text{if } |x| < 1 \\
    |x| - 0.5 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The Smooth L1 loss combines the benefits of L1 and L2 losses. It is less sensitive to outliers than L2 (preventing large errors from dominating gradients) while being smooth near zero (enabling stable optimization).

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}
To validate our proposed method, we design an evaluation framework based on synthetic yet realistic data. This section details the experimental environment, the baselines used for comparison, and the training procedures.

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Dataset}
Our evaluation is centered on a simulated 4 km $\times$ 4 km outdoor area in Çorum Province, Turkey, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_area}. This region is chosen to motivate the need for GNSS-denied solutions, as public reports indicate it is an area of frequent GPS interference~\cite{r14}.

Using a MATLAB-based data generator, we create a dataset of 1000 unique graph instances. For each instance, we place 64 nodes at a height of 1.0 m, of which 16 anchor nodes are each placed at a random position within a designated 1 km $\times$ 1 km grid cell and 48 unknown nodes are sampled uniformly at random across the full area. To model the physical environment, we generate data under two distinct propagation regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A realistic \textbf{terrain-aware} regime using the Longley-Rice model, which accounts for signal attenuation and shadowing from the area's actual terrain profile. This is our primary evaluation setting.
    \item An idealized \textbf{free-space} regime, which assumes no obstacles or terrain effects. This is included to highlight how environmental factors can dramatically alter the performance ranking of different localization methods.
\end{itemize}

For each directed pair of nodes in a graph, we simulate a time series of K=10 RSSI samples to capture realistic signal variability. Fig.~\ref{fig:rssi_variance} visualizes the stark difference in the RSSI-distance relationship between the two propagation regimes. The full dataset of 1000 graphs is split into a training set of 800 graphs and a held-out test set of 200 graphs. All GCN-based models are trained on the training set and evaluated on the test set. \subsection{Baselines}
We compare our proposed model against two strong baselines representing classical and standard deep learning approaches.

\subsubsection{Iterative Multilateration}
This classical geometric method represents the standard approach to network localization without machine learning. It first performs path-loss calibration and distance conversion, then iterates position solving and anchor promotion for 10 iterations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Path-Loss Estimation:} A simple log-distance model of the form $\mathrm{RSSI}(d) = A - 10n\log_{10}(d)$ is fit to anchor-anchor links, where true distances are known from anchor coordinates. Least-squares regression determines the optimal parameters $(A, n)$ for the current graph instance. This per-graph calibration allows the model to adapt to varying propagation conditions across different instances.
    \item \textbf{Distance Conversion:} The fitted path-loss model is applied to convert the mean RSSI of all other links (anchor-unknown and unknown-unknown) to distance estimates $\hat{d}$.
    \item \textbf{Position Solving:} For each unknown node that has at least 3 neighbors with known positions (anchors or previously localized nodes), the 2D coordinates are estimated using bounded nonlinear least-squares optimization. Bounds constrain solutions to lie within the deployment area.
    \item \textbf{Anchor Promotion:} Nodes that are successfully localized with residual error below a threshold are temporarily promoted to anchor status. Their estimated positions are used as references in subsequent iterations, extending the reach of localization to nodes that lack direct anchor connectivity.
\end{enumerate}
This method requires no training data and can be applied directly to new environments. However, anchor promotion is vulnerable to error propagation. If an early iteration produces an inaccurate position estimate, that error propagates to dependent nodes in later iterations, potentially causing cascading failures.

\subsubsection{Plain GCN Ablation}
This ablation study isolates the contribution of the physics-aware RSSI-to-distance module by removing it from the proposed architecture. The plain GCN uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Identical architecture:} Same number of NNConv layers, hidden dimensions, and EdgeNet structure.
    \item \textbf{Identical training procedure:} Same optimizer, learning rate, epochs, and loss function.
    \item \textbf{Modified edge features:} Only the raw RSSI time series $[r_{ij}^{(1)}, \ldots, r_{ij}^{(K)}]$, without the learned distance estimate $\hat{d}_{ij}$.
\end{itemize}
By comparing the proposed method against this ablation, we can quantify the benefit of incorporating physics-aware distance estimation into the edge features. The plain GCN must learn the relationship between raw RSSI values and geometric distances entirely from data, without the prior knowledge provided by the path-loss model.

\subsection{Training and Implementation Details}
All GCN models are implemented in PyTorch Geometric. We standardize all node features and target coordinates using scalers fit on the training data and inverse-transform the predictions for evaluation. The models are trained end-to-end using the Adam optimizer to minimize the Smooth L1 loss on the unknown nodes' coordinates, with weight decay applied to both the GCN and path-loss parameters, along with gradient clipping for stability. \subsection{Free-Space Results}
To highlight the impact of the propagation environment, we conduct the same evaluation under idealized free-space conditions. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:free_results}, the performance ranking of the methods changes substantially. Iterative multilateration, which benefits from a perfectly specified geometric model in this setting, achieves a very low mean error of 6.28 m, rising from worst to best. Our proposed GCN achieves a mean error of 51.15 m, while the plain GCN remains the weakest at 257.80 m. The error distributions in Figs.~\ref{fig:cdf_free} and \ref{fig:hist_free} confirm this trend. This result underscores the critical importance of using terrain-aware models for evaluation, as performance in free-space can be a misleading indicator of real-world performance.

\section{UAV Localization and Navigation Feasibility}
\label{sec:system_eval}
The self-localization results above show that our method achieves a mean beacon position error of approximately 206 m. To assess whether this accuracy is sufficient for coarse navigation tasks, we evaluate the complete positioning pipeline by combining the proposed GCN with the client-side UAV localization method of \cite{r1}, which uses robust multilateration with Huber loss and altitude constraints. We analyze how beacon position errors propagate to UAV localization through sensitivity analysis and demonstrate navigation feasibility through closed-loop simulations.

\subsection{Simulation Framework}
The simulation uses RSSI data generated from the same 4 km $\times$ 4 km deployment area with 64 beacons. We construct an RSSI interpolation model from Longley-Rice simulated measurements on a 400 $\times$ 400 grid (10 m spacing), enabling evaluation of UAV localization at any continuous position within the deployment area. The UAV operates at a fixed altitude of 100 m, and the multilateration algorithm from \cite{r1}, which uses Huber loss with altitude constraint, is applied using the $k=10$ beacons with strongest RSSI.

To model the effect of infrastructure self-localization errors, we add Gaussian noise to beacon coordinates. The noise is characterized by mean 2D error rather than per-axis standard deviation, providing direct comparability with localization error metrics. For a target mean 2D error of $\mu$, the per-axis standard deviation is $\sigma = \mu / \sqrt{\pi/2} \approx 0.798\mu$, derived from the Rayleigh distribution of 2D distances from independent Gaussian components.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
We analyze how beacon position errors propagate to UAV localization accuracy by randomly sampling 2,000 positions from the RSSI grid and computing localization error at each position for varying levels of beacon position uncertainty.

\label{fig:sensitivity}

Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} shows how UAV localization error changes with beacon position uncertainty. Key findings include:

\paragraph{Baseline Error}
Even with perfect beacon positions, UAV localization exhibits a baseline error of approximately 122 m due to inherent RSSI measurement noise. This baseline represents the fundamental limitation of RSSI-based multilateration in the simulated terrain environment. The influence of beacon position error becomes apparent only when it approaches or exceeds this baseline level.

\paragraph{Moderate Error Propagation}
Beyond the baseline, UAV localization error increases approximately linearly with beacon position error, but at a rate less than 1:1. At 200 m beacon error (approximately matching GCN performance), mean UAV error reaches 157 m, an increase of only 35 m from the baseline. Importantly, the system does not exhibit catastrophic failure modes. Even at 300 m beacon error, significantly worse than expected from the GCN, UAV localization remains functional with mean error of 207 m.

\paragraph{Tail Behavior}
The 90th percentile (P90) error grows faster than the mean, indicating that beacon errors more severely impact geometrically unfavorable positions. At 200 m beacon error, P90 reaches 287 m, meaning 90\% of positions achieve better than this accuracy.

\subsection{Navigation Feasibility}
To evaluate whether the system supports practical navigation, we conduct closed-loop navigation simulations across three mission profiles, including diagonal transit (point-to-point across 4 km), circular orbit (1 km radius, 8 waypoints), and area sweep (zigzag coverage pattern).

The UAV uses a simplified kinematic model with proportional heading control. At each time step ($\Delta t = 1$ s), the controller computes the desired heading based on the estimated position and target waypoint:
\begin{equation}
\theta = \arctan2(y_{\text{wp}} - \hat{y}, \, x_{\text{wp}} - \hat{x})
\end{equation}
where $(x_{\text{wp}}, y_{\text{wp}})$ is the waypoint position and $(\hat{x}, \hat{y})$ is the estimated UAV position from multilateration. The UAV moves toward this heading at a constant speed of 4.5 m/s. Waypoints are considered reached when the UAV's estimated position is within 200 m, preventing indefinite circling near waypoints due to position uncertainty.

\paragraph{Heading Error Bound}
Navigation remains stable despite high localization error due to a geometric property of the heading computation. Assuming the worst case where the position error is perpendicular to the true heading direction, the maximum heading error is bounded by:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\theta_{\max} \approx \arctan\left(\frac{\epsilon}{d}\right)
\label{eq:heading_error}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is the localization error (Euclidean distance between estimated and true position) and $d$ is the distance to the target waypoint. Fig.~\ref{fig:heading_error} illustrates this relationship. At 1 km distance, 150 m position error produces a worst-case heading error of only 8.5 degrees, enabling reliable long-range navigation. At 200 m distance (approaching the waypoint threshold), heading error reaches 37 degrees, explaining why the 200 m threshold is necessary to avoid the unstable close-approach phase.

\paragraph{Operational Scope}
The system reliably supports long-range transit between waypoints separated by more than 1 km, orbit missions with moderate waypoint spacing (500--1000 m), and area coverage with planned overlap to compensate for navigation uncertainty. The system is unsuitable for precision landing, dense waypoint sequences with spacing below 300 m, or obstacle avoidance requiring precise position knowledge.

\section{Discussion}
We analyze the key factors behind the observed performance differences, discuss limitations, and identify directions for future work.

\subsection{Performance Drivers}
The substantial performance gap between methods originates from how they handle the ill-posed RSSI-to-distance conversion in terrain-rich environments.

\paragraph{Why Multilateration Fails}
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rssi_variance}, terrain effects create a noisy, multi-modal RSSI distribution where a single RSSI value can correspond to multiple distances. Iterative multilateration commits to hard distance estimates for each link, producing a geometrically inconsistent constraint system. When many of these distance estimates are wrong, the optimization has no valid solution, resulting in the catastrophic outliers visible in the error distribution of Fig.~\ref{fig:hist_nonfree}.

\paragraph{Why the Proposed Method Succeeds}
Our method succeeds by reframing the problem. Instead of committing to hard distance conversion, the trainable path-loss module generates distance estimates that serve as soft, physically-grounded priors. The edge-conditioned convolution learns to weigh this prior against the raw RSSI time series on a per-edge basis, effectively learning when to trust the distance estimate and when to distrust it. 

Furthermore, the GCN architecture provides implicit regularization through message passing. Whereas multilateration computes each node's position independently, the GCN performs collective, simultaneous inference across the entire graph. Each node's position is constrained by its neighbors, smoothing predictions and preventing isolated large errors. This graph-based reasoning contributes to the reduction in the error tail, as reflected in the P90 and P95 columns of Table~\ref{tab:main_results}.

\paragraph{Role of Physics-Aware Features}
The comparison with the plain GCN (72\% error reduction) demonstrates the importance of the learned distance estimate. Without physical priors, the network must learn the relationship between raw RSSI values and distances purely from data, which is difficult given the complex, terrain-dependent mapping. By encoding an approximate physical relationship into the edge features, the physics-aware module reduces the burden on the network, resulting in the 72\% error reduction observed on the test set.

\paragraph{Learned Parameter Interpretability}
After training on the terrain-aware dataset, the model learns path-loss exponent $n = 4.86$ and offset $o = -1.82$. The high path-loss exponent (compared to the free-space value of $n = 2$) reflects the severe signal attenuation caused by the mountainous terrain, including diffraction losses over ridges and shadowing effects. This learned value aligns with empirical path-loss models for obstructed outdoor environments, which typically report exponents in the range of 3--5 for mountainous or shadowed terrain. The ability to examine these learned values provides interpretable insight into the effective propagation conditions of the deployment environment.

\paragraph{Beacon Hardware Variance}
Manufacturing tolerances cause slight differences between beacons, such as 1--2 dB variation in transmission power. However, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rssi_variance}, terrain-induced RSSI variance spans approximately 70--80 dB for a given distance, making hardware variations negligible by comparison. The dominant error source remains terrain-induced propagation effects, not hardware differences between beacons.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
While the results are promising, several limitations present opportunities for future work.

\paragraph{Absolute Accuracy}
The proposed method does not achieve GNSS-level accuracy. Mean error of 207 m is sufficient for many applications (regional navigation, search area narrowing) but insufficient for precision tasks. Future work should investigate the trade-off between beacon density and achievable accuracy.

\paragraph{Strategic Beacon Placement}
Our simulations use grid-constrained anchor placement and uniformly random unknown node placement without optimizing deployment strategy. In practice, beacon placement relative to terrain strongly influences localization performance, because line-of-sight (LOS) links produce RSSI values that follow the path-loss model more closely, yielding more accurate distance estimates, while NLOS links exhibit severe attenuation and high variance that degrade distance accuracy. Maximizing the proportion of LOS links in the network graph is therefore a key deployment consideration.

In mountainous terrain, beacons on hilltops or ridges maintain LOS connections to more neighbors, while beacons in valleys connect only to nearby nodes on the same valley floor, creating regions of sparse connectivity. Although the edge-conditioned convolutions can learn to down-weight unreliable NLOS links, this mitigation is insufficient when most links to a node are NLOS. For anchors specifically, a relevant scenario is placing them only around the perimeter of a GNSS-denied region, which may exhibit different error characteristics for interior nodes far from all anchors. Systematic investigation of placement optimization under terrain and accessibility constraints remains a direction for future work.

\paragraph{Simulation-to-Reality Gap}
Both training and evaluation use simulated data. While Longley-Rice provides realistic terrain effects, it does not capture all real-world complexities such as device heterogeneity, antenna placement variations, temporal environmental changes, and interference. Bridging this gap, for example by fine-tuning the simulation-trained model on a small set of real-world measurements or by conducting field experiments, is a critical next step.

\paragraph{Global Path-Loss Parameters}
The model learns global path-loss parameters applied uniformly across the network. Real deployments may exhibit regional variations in propagation characteristics. Spatially-aware or per-region parameterization could improve accuracy in heterogeneous environments.

\paragraph{Terrain-Specific Training}
The model captures terrain effects implicitly through RSSI statistics rather than receiving terrain data as explicit input, so the learned propagation patterns are specific to the terrain used for training. Deploying in a new region requires regenerating training data using terrain-aware propagation simulation for that region and retraining the model, because each terrain produces distinct propagation characteristics that the model cannot generalize from one region to another. A promising direction for future work is developing a foundation model pre-trained on diverse terrain conditions, which could then be fine-tuned for a new deployment environment without full retraining.

\paragraph{Computational Cost}
Generating terrain-specific training data requires running propagation simulations for all pairwise links, and the computational cost grows with area size and number of beacons. However, each link can be simulated independently, making the process straightforward to parallelize. In practice, physical beacon deployment (site access, installation, power provisioning) is likely a greater bottleneck than the computational preparation.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper addresses the problem of beacon self-localization in sparse, outdoor LPWA networks using only RSSI. We introduce a physics-aware, edge-conditioned GCN that integrates a trainable RSSI-to-distance module learned jointly with the graph network. This approach provides a robust physical prior while leveraging unknown-to-unknown connectivity, keeping anchor nodes fixed to prevent drift.

In terrain-aware simulations, our model achieves a mean error of 206.74 m, outperforming a plain GCN (750.87 m) and iterative multilateration (1974.40 m), reducing both typical and extreme errors. Our results also demonstrate that idealized free-space evaluations can be misleading, as they substantially change the performance ranking of the methods.

Combining this infrastructure self-localization with the client-side UAV positioning method from \cite{r1}, we show that 200 m beacon error produces only 157 m mean UAV localization error, and closed-loop navigation simulations achieve 100\% waypoint success across diverse mission profiles. The heading error bound (\ref{eq:heading_error}) ensures that heading errors remain small when targets are distant, enabling reliable waypoint navigation despite localization errors substantially larger than GNSS.

Future work will study accuracy-density trade-offs, robustness to anchor geometry, sim-to-real adaptation, and foundation models for cross-terrain generalization. These results suggest that physically-grounded graph learning, paired with terrain-aware simulation, provides a promising foundation for outdoor localization in GNSS-denied environments.
